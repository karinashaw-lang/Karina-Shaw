## Genuine findings

- **The statutory "joint employer" hook is textual, not judge-made.**
  The wage order's "employer" definition reaches a person who "directly
  or indirectly, or through an agent or any other person, employs **or
  exercises control over** the wages, hours, or working conditions of
  any person." The disjunctive and the "indirectly / through an agent"
  language are what allow the definition to describe two businesses at
  once, and Martinez says so in terms: the definition "by its terms
  imposes liability on multiple entities who divide among themselves
  control over those different aspects of the employment relationship."
- **California did not adopt the federal test.** Martinez expressly
  declined to read the FLSA "economic reality" test into the wage
  orders and noted that no wage order incorporates federal law in
  defining "employ" or "employer." That is the reason the sheet says
  nothing about federal joint-employer standards.
- **Misclassification and joint employment are different questions.**
  Curry draws the line explicitly: in joint employment "the alleged
  employee is already considered an employee of the primary employer;
  the issue is whether the employee is also an employee of the alleged
  secondary employer," so the policy rationale for the ABC burden-shift
  does not carry over. The clause reports that this is a Court of
  Appeal conclusion phrased in terms of what the Supreme Court "appears
  to have intended," not a Supreme Court holding.
- **Patterson is not a franchisor immunity rule.** The opinion's own
  closing passage states that a franchisor "will be liable if it has
  retained or assumed the right of general control over the relevant
  day-to-day operations," and that its holding "is limited to
  determining the circumstances under which an employment or agency
  relationship exists as a prerequisite to pursuing statutory and tort
  theories like those alleged against the franchisor here." Both are
  quoted or disclosed rather than smoothed over.
- **The Goonewardene sentence is the lower court's holding, not the
  Supreme Court's.** The Supreme Court granted review on third party
  beneficiary, negligence and negligent-misrepresentation theories, and
  the "payroll company cannot properly be considered an employer"
  language appears in its statement of the case describing the Court of
  Appeal. The clause and its gap say this expressly, and Futrell —
  which did so hold — carries the substantive quotes.
- **§ 2810.3 does not displace this analysis.** Subdivision (f) says
  the shared liability is "supplemental of" other statutory and common
  law theories, which is why falling below its 25-worker threshold does
  not answer the wage-order question.
- **Raines reaches hiring directly.** The certified question arose from
  pre-employment medical screenings performed by an outside provider
  for prospective employers, so FEHA agent liability is a hiring-stage
  route by which a second business becomes an "employer."

## Honest gap(s) disclosed

that Wage Order No. 4-2001 is one of seventeen orders and the

footnote-reference marker interrupts it
(`jointemp_martinez_three_definitions`,
`jointemp_martinez_multiple_entities`); that Martinez supplies no
formula for allocating control and does not decide how a federal
joint-employer question would come out
(`jointemp_martinez_multiple_entities`); that the "failure to hinder"

star-pagination marker ("*312") inside that sentence so the quotation

(`jointemp_martinez_suffer_or_permit`); that Patterson arose on summary

(`jointemp_payroll_company_not_employer`); that § 2810.3 is quoted only

## Method

**No CourtListener MCP tool was called at any point.** All case-law
access went through curl via the environment proxy.

**Regulation — two independent Department of Industrial Relations
printings.** The wage order definitions were verified against two
different files published by DIR, each fetched with a different
User-Agent:

- `https://www.dir.ca.gov/iwc/IWCArticle4.pdf` — the current printing
  (IWC Form 1104, Rev. 11/2018), HTTP 200, 3,291,219 bytes, 11 pages.
- `https://www.dir.ca.gov/IWC/WageOrders2006/IWCArticle4.pdf` — DIR's
  2006 printing of the same order, HTTP 200, 355,151 bytes.

These are separate files with independently produced text layers
(the current printing renders the typographic quotation marks around
defined terms as the characters "―" and "‖"; the 2006 printing renders
them as straight ASCII quotation marks). The § 2(E), 2(F) and 2(H)
definitions are word-for-word identical in both, which is what makes
the comparison meaningful rather than a re-read of one file.
`govt.westlaw.com/calregs` returned only a 4,777-byte JavaScript shell
and was not relied on.

**Statutes — two fetches each with distinct User-Agent strings** from
`leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml`:
Lab. Code § 18 (163,062 bytes both fetches), Lab. Code § 2810.3
(172,083 / 171,379 bytes — the difference is the per-request JSF
ViewState token, not the statutory text), and Gov. Code § 12926
(182,367 bytes both fetches). Extracted statutory text was compared
with `diff` and is identical across fetches for all three sections.

**Case law — opinion pages fetched twice, then phrase-verified against
CourtListener's search index as a second path.** The unauthenticated
v4 search JSON (`/api/rest/v4/search/?type=o&q=...`) was used once to
locate Martinez and Patterson cluster ids and then returned HTTP 429
("Request was throttled. Expected available in 7544 seconds") for the
rest of the session. The **public search feed**
(`/feed/search/?type=o&q=...`) was not throttled and was used both to
locate the remaining cluster ids and, per the task brief's option (d),
as the phrase-level second verification path.
