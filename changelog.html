What changed, and why

Every entry below is a real defect this project found in its own content, and the fix — not a marketing page, an audit trail. Most legal-document sites don't publish this because they don't have it: nothing gets cited, so nothing can be checked, so nothing gets caught. ← Back to Groundtruth

A note on one entry below: in late August 2026 this project made a deliberate policy decision to keep clause wording jurisdiction-general rather than naming specific states as examples in the document text — the underlying real citations stayed, but several clauses were reworded and one (a California-specific independent-contractor test) was removed outright. That's recorded here too, since it changed what several "Verified" badges actually say.
2026-08-29 Reasonable Accommodation · Employee Handbook

A clause overstated the law it cited

What we found The clause claimed a bad-faith failure to engage in the interactive process "can itself be a basis for liability, independent of whether an accommodation actually existed." Reading the clause's own cited case in full — not just the fragment already quoted — surfaced a four-part test whose fourth element is exactly what the clause said didn't matter: the employee generally still has to show a reasonable accommodation could have been identified but for the employer's lack of good faith.
What changed Rewrote the clause to state the accommodation requirement accurately, rewrote the gap to explain the correction, and added the four-part-test quote itself as a supporting citation.
Taylor v. Phoenixville School District, 184 F.3d 296 (3d Cir. 1999)
2026-08-29 Work Authorization · Employee Handbook

An anti-discrimination claim didn't disclose who it doesn't cover

What we found The clause said the same I-9 documentary requirements apply "to every employee, regardless of national origin or citizenship status." Reading past the already-cited fragment of the statute turned up 8 U.S.C. §1324b(a)(2)(A): employers with three or fewer employees are exempt from this anti-discrimination provision entirely. Separately, the "three business days" I-9 deadline itself isn't universal — hires for under three business days require same-day completion.
What changed Added both provisions as citations and extended the gap to disclose the small-employer exemption and the same-day exception.
8 U.S.C. § 1324b(a)(2)(A) 8 C.F.R. § 274a.2(b)(1)(iii)

2026-08-29 Accrued Vacation / PTO · Termination Letter

A "can't be forfeited" rule didn't distinguish forfeiture from a cap

What we found The clause said accrued vacation, once vested, "can't be forfeited." True — but that's often misread as meaning an employer can't cap how much vacation accrues going forward, which is a different question with a different answer.
What changed Added a case confirming the actual line: forbidding forfeiture of time already earned doesn't require unlimited future accrual — a policy that stops the clock on earning more once a threshold is banked is a different thing from taking away time already vested.
Boothby v. Atlas Mechanical, Inc., 6 Cal. App. 4th 1595 (1992)
2026-08-29 Independent Contractor Agreement — policy change

Generalized away from named-state examples

What changed Several clauses across the corpus previously illustrated real state-law splits by naming specific states (a California test, an Ohio-vs-Washington split, a Montana exception). On explicit direction to keep the project general rather than state-specific, those clauses were reworded to state the general principle without picking an illustrative state, and one clause whose entire content was a single state's specific rule — with no general version worth keeping — was removed rather than watered down into something that no longer said anything real.
The honest tradeoff This is a real loss of specificity, not a pure improvement. A clause that used to name a state's actual exception now says "this varies — check your state," which is true but less useful than the version that named the exception. Recorded here rather than quietly dropped.
2026-08-31 Exempt / Non-Exempt Classification Notice — caught before shipping

Almost reintroduced a named-state example into a federal-law clause

What almost happened While auditing the corpus for missed specific detail, a new clause citing California's daily-overtime statute was drafted and added to the federal overtime-classification document, to illustrate that some states require more than the federal floor.
Why it was reverted That's the same pattern the entry above deliberately removed: a named-state illustrative example bolted onto a clause whose actual content is federal law. The Business Formation and Real Estate documents added later do cite a specific state's statutes at length, but for a different reason — those documents govern subjects with no federal counterpart (LLCs, corporations, leases are creatures of state law), so a real state's statute is the only way to cite anything concrete at all, and every such citation is explicitly framed as one state's evidence, not a jurisdiction claim. Bolting a state example onto an otherwise-federal clause is the older, different problem this project already decided against. Caught and reverted before the commit that would have shipped it.

What we found The clause said Probate Code § 10150(a), (b), and (c) "each" require court permission on a showing of necessity and advantage, but only subdivision (c) carries that requirement.
What changed Revised the summary sentence to attribute the court-permission requirement to subdivision (c) alone.

2026-10-31 Section 203 Penalty Cross-Reference · Final Wage Payment for Events Employees at Professional Baseball Venues (Labor Code § 201.8) — Information Sheet

A penalty cross-reference clause skipped an interpretive step it claimed to avoid

What we found The clause said a violation of § 201.8’s next-payday rule triggers the § 203 penalty "without depending on" any interpretive step, but § 203(a) is textually limited to a discharged/quitting employee, while § 201.8 governs the opposite (non-discharge) scenario.
What changed Revised the sentence to acknowledge the interpretive step the argument actually requires.
