What changed, and why

Every entry below is a real defect this project found in its own content, and the fix — not a marketing page, an audit trail. Most legal-document sites don't publish this because they don't have it: nothing gets cited, so nothing can be checked, so nothing gets caught. ← Back to Groundtruth

A note on one entry below: in late August 2026 this project made a deliberate policy decision to keep clause wording jurisdiction-general rather than naming specific states as examples in the document text — the underlying real citations stayed, but several clauses were reworded and one (a California-specific independent-contractor test) was removed outright. That's recorded here too, since it changed what several "Verified" badges actually say.
2026-08-29 Reasonable Accommodation · Employee Handbook

A clause overstated the law it cited

What we found The clause claimed a bad-faith failure to engage in the interactive process "can itself be a basis for liability, independent of whether an accommodation actually existed." Reading the clause's own cited case in full — not just the fragment already quoted — surfaced a four-part test whose fourth element is exactly what the clause said didn't matter: the employee generally still has to show a reasonable accommodation could have been identified but for the employer's lack of good faith.
What changed Rewrote the clause to state the accommodation requirement accurately, rewrote the gap to explain the correction, and added the four-part-test quote itself as a supporting citation.
Taylor v. Phoenixville School District, 184 F.3d 296 (3d Cir. 1999)
2026-08-29 Work Authorization · Employee Handbook

An anti-discrimination claim didn't disclose who it doesn't cover

What we found The clause said the same I-9 documentary requirements apply "to every employee, regardless of national origin or citizenship status." Reading past the already-cited fragment of the statute turned up 8 U.S.C. §1324b(a)(2)(A): employers with three or fewer employees are exempt from this anti-discrimination provision entirely. Separately, the "three business days" I-9 deadline itself isn't universal — hires for under three business days require same-day completion.
What changed Added both provisions as citations and extended the gap to disclose the small-employer exemption and the same-day exception.
8 U.S.C. § 1324b(a)(2)(A) 8 C.F.R. § 274a.2(b)(1)(iii)
2026-08-29 Timekeeping · Employee Handbook

A recordkeeping claim was citing the wrong statute

What we found The clause's recordkeeping-duty sentence ("non-exempt employees must record all time worked") was backed only by 29 U.S.C. §207(a)(1) — the overtime pay-rate rule, not the actual recordkeeping duty, which is a separate provision.
What changed Fetched 29 U.S.C. §211(c) directly and added it as the precise citation for the recordkeeping sentence; kept §207(a)(1) for the underlying pay obligation it still supports.
29 U.S.C. § 211(c)
2026-08-29 Ownership of Work Product · Invention Assignment Agreement

A copyright citation named the wrong provision

What we found 17 U.S.C. §101 only defines the term "work made for hire" — it doesn't vest anything. The provision that actually vests ownership in the employer, and carries its own written-instrument carve-out, is §201(b).
What changed Added §201(b) as a citation and explained in the gap why a written assignment still matters even for work that already qualifies as made for hire.
17 U.S.C. § 201(b)
2026-08-29 Continuation of Health Coverage (COBRA) · Termination Letter

A clause promised continuation coverage with no actual duration

What we found The clause said COBRA coverage lasts "temporarily" without ever stating how long — a vague promise instead of the concrete, checkable fact the rest of this project holds itself to.
What changed Fetched the actual maximum-coverage-period provision and rewrote the clause to state the real figure: 18 months, extendable to 36 for a second qualifying event.
29 U.S.C. § 1162(2)(A)(i)
2026-08-29 Accrued Vacation / PTO · Termination Letter

A "can't be forfeited" rule didn't distinguish forfeiture from a cap

What we found The clause said accrued vacation, once vested, "can't be forfeited." True — but that's often misread as meaning an employer can't cap how much vacation accrues going forward, which is a different question with a different answer.
What changed Added a case confirming the actual line: forbidding forfeiture of time already earned doesn't require unlimited future accrual — a policy that stops the clock on earning more once a threshold is banked is a different thing from taking away time already vested.
Boothby v. Atlas Mechanical, Inc., 6 Cal. App. 4th 1595 (1992)
2026-08-29 Independent Contractor Agreement — policy change

Generalized away from named-state examples

What changed Several clauses across the corpus previously illustrated real state-law splits by naming specific states (a California test, an Ohio-vs-Washington split, a Montana exception). On explicit direction to keep the project general rather than state-specific, those clauses were reworded to state the general principle without picking an illustrative state, and one clause whose entire content was a single state's specific rule — with no general version worth keeping — was removed rather than watered down into something that no longer said anything real.
The honest tradeoff This is a real loss of specificity, not a pure improvement. A clause that used to name a state's actual exception now says "this varies — check your state," which is true but less useful than the version that named the exception. Recorded here rather than quietly dropped.
2026-08-31 Exempt / Non-Exempt Classification Notice — caught before shipping

Almost reintroduced a named-state example into a federal-law clause

What almost happened While auditing the corpus for missed specific detail, a new clause citing California's daily-overtime statute was drafted and added to the federal overtime-classification document, to illustrate that some states require more than the federal floor.
Why it was reverted That's the same pattern the entry above deliberately removed: a named-state illustrative example bolted onto a clause whose actual content is federal law. The Business Formation and Real Estate documents added later do cite a specific state's statutes at length, but for a different reason — those documents govern subjects with no federal counterpart (LLCs, corporations, leases are creatures of state law), so a real state's statute is the only way to cite anything concrete at all, and every such citation is explicitly framed as one state's evidence, not a jurisdiction claim. Bolting a state example onto an otherwise-federal clause is the older, different problem this project already decided against. Caught and reverted before the commit that would have shipped it.
2026-09-22 Waiting-Time Penalty Deadline · Wage-and-Hour Claim Filing Deadlines

A quote presented the source's sentences out of order

What we found The clause's stored quote led with the general one-year-penalty rule and ended with the sentence stating the actual holding ("Section 203(b) sets forth a single limitations period..."), reading as if the court built up to that conclusion. The opinion states that sentence first, then the supporting reasoning — the stored quote had reordered it.
What changed Reordered the quote to match the source, marking the now-non-contiguous span with an ellipsis rather than presenting reordered text as one continuous passage.
Pineda v. Bank of America, N.A., 50 Cal.4th 1389 (2010)
2026-09-22 Benson: No "Part Performance" Exception · Transmutation of Marital Property

A misplaced parenthesis changed what the quote said was parenthetical

What we found The stored quote read "...the general statute of frauds (Civ.Code, § 1624, subd. (a) (Civil Code section 1624(a))), in which..." — an extra open parenthesis folded "Civil Code section 1624(a)" inside the statute citation as if the court had nested it there. The source keeps them as two separate parentheticals.
What changed Corrected the parenthesis placement to match the source exactly: "(Civ. Code, § 1624, subd. (a)) (Civil Code section 1624(a))."
In re Marriage of Benson, 36 Cal.4th 1096 (2005)
2026-09-29 Work Authorization · Employee Handbook

A project-added citation was folded inside the court's own quotation marks

What we found The stored quote read: an employer "must within three business days of hire … [c]omplete [S]ection 2" — 8 C.F.R. §274a.2(b)(1)(ii). The regulation cite after the dash reads as part of what the court wrote, inside the same quoted sentence, when it's this project's own supporting citation for the point the quote makes — the opinion doesn't write its citation that way at that spot.
What changed Trimmed the quote to the words the court actually wrote. The regulation is still cited for this clause — just not folded inside the quotation marks as if the court put it there.
Split Rail Fence Co. v. United States, 852 F.3d 1228 (10th Cir. 2017)
2026-09-29 Securing Live Testimony; Good-Cause Finding · Live Testimony at Family Law Hearings

Two citation links pointed at a different case entirely

What we found A full re-fetch of every CourtListener-hosted citation turned up a stale cluster id: 2 of this document's 6 citations to In re Marriage of George & Deamon still pointed at a CourtListener URL that now resolves to an unrelated 1992 U.S. Tax Court opinion. Clicking either "Verified" badge would have taken a reader to the wrong case. The other 4 citations in the same document already had the correct URL.
What changed Located the opinion's current id by searching its own citation and updated both stale URLs to match the 4 that were already correct. The quoted text itself was accurate throughout — only the links were wrong.
In re Marriage of George & Deamon, 35 Cal.App.5th 476 (2019)
2026-09-30 "Naked Licensing" and the Risk of Losing the Mark · Trademark License Agreement

A quote spliced three sentences together and silently dropped what sat between them

What we found The stored quote read as one continuous passage, but the opinion actually states it as three separately-quoted sentences with the court's own case and treatise citations sitting between them. The stored quote skipped straight from one quoted sentence to the next with no ellipsis marking the gap — reading as a verbatim contiguous quote when it wasn't one. It had also silently resolved two of the source's bracketed mid-quote recapitalizations ("[a]" and "[u]ncontrolled") to plain capital letters, dropping the brackets a direct quote should keep.
What changed Rewrote the quote to mark both elisions with "..." and restored the source's own quotation marks and bracket notation. The underlying legal claim was accurate throughout; only the quote's presentation as unbroken, unbracketed text was wrong.
Barcamerica International USA Trust v. Tyfield Importers, Inc., 289 F.3d 589 (9th Cir. 2002)
2026-09-29 Six clauses across four documents — minor quote-accuracy corrections

A font's typographic characters and a spaced ellipsis didn't survive being typed in as plain text

What we found A from-scratch re-fetch of every CourtListener-hosted citation (187 opinions, 1,004 quotes) turned up six quotes with a small, shared pattern of transcription slips rather than substantive errors: an ASCII double hyphen ("--") typed in place of the source's single em dash, in two quotes from Yvanova v. New Century Mortgage Corp. and one each from Sciarratta v. U.S. Bank National Assn. and Salas v. Sierra Chemical Co.; the word "postdiscovery" split into "post-discovery" in a second Salas quote; the word "twenty" standing in for the source's numeral "20" in a Benson quote; and a spaced ellipsis (" . . . ") in a Murphy v. Kenneth Cole Productions quote that this project's own checker didn't recognize as an elision marker, so re-verification reported it as an exact-match failure until the formatting was corrected.
What changed Corrected each quote to match its source exactly. None of these changed what any clause claims the law is — each was a character-for-character transcription detail that the re-verification pass exists specifically to catch.
Yvanova v. New Century Mortgage Corp., 62 Cal.4th 919 (2016) Sciarratta v. U.S. Bank National Assn., 247 Cal.App.4th 552 (2016) Salas v. Sierra Chemical Co., 59 Cal.4th 407 (2014) In re Marriage of Benson, 36 Cal.4th 1096 (2005) Murphy v. Kenneth Cole Productions, Inc., 40 Cal.4th 1094 (2007)
